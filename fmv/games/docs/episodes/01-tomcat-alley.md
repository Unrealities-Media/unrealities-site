# Episode 1: Tomcat Alley

Published practice episode 1 · R.I.P. FMV · Race to the Credits · planned, not recorded

**Episode question:** Can I beat a fighter-jet action movie from the back seat with a D-pad?

**Recommended title:** I Tried to Beat a Fighter-Jet Movie — Tomcat Alley

**Main version:** North American Sega CD, English, original disc and standard controller. **Challenge:** finish the campaign with the game's normal retries allowed, then attempt a continuous personal speed-clear if the first completion leaves a worthwhile improvement story. **Edit target:** 12–15 minutes, with permission to finish shorter if the story is complete.

The comedy comes from taking the job seriously while learning how little of the aircraft you actually control. Let the game surprise you, demonstrate a specific mistake, and show whether practice fixes it. This is a published first episode, not a disposable rehearsal. Budget and existing ownership do not determine the version choice.

**First recording session:** log the disc and hardware → test boot/intro and isolated audio → record the genuine first mission attempt → inspect the saved sample → read the mission guidance and record practice → log the result. Keep all first-exposure footage. Leave the final narration, timer target and optional PC comparison until the actual playthrough gives them a reason to exist.

## Best version for this episode

| Edition | Use | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **North American Sega CD, standard pad** | **Main challenge and primary footage** | The original console interface makes the premise legible: a movie plays while you manage its commands with a D-pad. It gives the episode a clear identity and an observable learning curve. |

| Sega CD emulation | Conditional fallback if a usable original purchase/hardware route cannot be obtained | Label the environment and keep its timing/results separate. Do not silently replace original-hardware footage with an emulator during the final attempt. |

The Sega CD recommendation is an editorial choice for this challenge, not a claim that it has the sharpest image of every edition. The Windows port is [documented as a 1995 release](https://www.myabandonware.com/game/tomcat-alley-fw2); its listing supplies a Windows ISO, while user comments report installation/unlock difficulties. Those reports are reasons to test the complete game, not proof that every copy has the same problem. A modern-PC installation and full-campaign capture route remain untested.

Use an English disc matching the console's region. Capture the disc label and identify the pressing in the recording log. The ordinary Sega CD release does not require a special 32X CD edition. Purchase routes and dated CIB references remain in the [catalog guide](../fmv-play-guide.md#124-tomcat-alley--titan-432); price is not part of the episode's selection argument.

For a useful PC comparison, record the same briefing, a radio/waypoint interaction and a combat scene. Label both editions and match their displayed size. Compare the footage you actually captured. If the PC setup does not produce a reliable full-game route, omit the segment; episode one already has a complete central challenge.

## The challenge and timing rules

Declare these as **R.I.P. FMV house rules**. They are not verified Speedrun.com category rules, and no leaderboard absence, world record or first-ever completion is claimed.

| Rule | Episode-one definition |
| --- | --- |
| Primary goal | Reach the campaign's successful ending and ensuing credits through normal progression. A game-over credits sequence is not a completion. |
| Initial state | Begin a new campaign, rather than a late-mission save or mission-selection code. Record the title/menu state and the chosen entry. |
| Settings | Use stock settings and record any exposed options. Do not invent a named difficulty if the installed edition offers none. Keep the same settings for comparison attempts. |
| Controller | Standard D-pad controller; no turbo or external input automation. |
| Retries | Normal in-game retries and continues are allowed. Count mission failures separately from full campaign attempts. This is not advertised as a no-death or one-credit challenge. |
| Practice help | After the first-exposure attempt, the manual, notes and a walkthrough may be used. Identify help that materially changes the route. No route cheats or debug codes in a scored attempt. |
| Saving/resuming | If normal saving exists in the actual pressing, it may support the learning playthrough. Disclose session boundaries. A later timed continuous attempt cannot load a save or splice sessions. Saving behavior still needs an on-hardware check. |
| Technical interruptions | Preserve the recording. Label a campaign assembled across sessions as a completion playthrough. A broken capture or reset means restarting the later continuous timed attempt. |

**Optional personal speed-clear:** start the clock on the input that begins the first mission briefing from a fresh campaign; stop on the first frame of credits **after the successful final ending**. Establish a reference image for that successful transition during the learning clear. Keep loading, failures, native retries and pauses in elapsed real time. A deliberate return to the title screen or reset ends the attempt. The manual permits Start to advance pre-mission scenes; allow that normal control in the speed-clear and declare that choice. Keep a complete uninterrupted source recording from before the starting input to after the stopping frame.

If the game's ending does not transition directly as expected, document the actual ending landmark before the scored attempt and use it consistently. Do not quietly change the timer endpoint after seeing the result.

**Success ladder:** a documented campaign completion is the main win; a cleaner or faster continuous clear is a stretch goal. Set no arbitrary sub-hour target before learning the game. If the project stalls, use the honest incomplete result, show the obstacle and the attempts to solve it, and change the title/verdict to match.

## What the viewer needs to understand

You play the Radar Intercept Officer; the pilot handles the flying. The manual's controls are D-pad for the reticle, A to fire, B to select HUD functions, C to show/hide the HUD, and Start to pause or advance pre-mission scenes. Radio, navigation and weapons therefore compete for your attention. [Sega manual, printed pp. 2, 4–9](https://r.mprd.se/Sega%20CD/Manuals/Tomcat%20Alley%20%28U%29.pdf).

Teach only three ideas before returning to the story: **answer the command, select the right HUD function, execute it in time**. Explain the rest when it causes an actual failure. Do not make the audience memorize the instrument panel.

For the post-blind investigation, the manual describes an initial five-second radio/waypoint response window followed by a further three seconds after a reprimand; it also warns that choosing waypoints out of order increases resistance. These are good experiments to check in your own footage, rather than dialogue or outcomes to stage. [Manual, printed pp. 6–7](https://r.mprd.se/Sega%20CD/Manuals/Tomcat%20Alley%20%28U%29.pdf).

Keep the mechanics distinct: B selects HUD items and an interception target; A fires once the weapon is ready. An annotated freeze-frame should explain which task you misunderstood. Avoid blaming the wrong button or a missed input on the game if the recording shows a player error.

## Recording plan

### 1. Prepare the capture without consuming the blind playthrough

Use Sega CD/Genesis video and correctly mixed game audio → compatible cables → RetroTINK-4K Pro → HDMI capture card → recording PC. Play from a low-latency display path rather than relying on a delayed recording preview. Confirm the console models, region, audio routing, scaler firmware and card model; the existing kit does not establish a tested chain.

Use OBS Studio as the proposed recorder. Start with a card-supported SDR output such as 1080p at 59.94/60 Hz for the North American setup, matching the actual HDMI output and device mode. This is a proposed capture profile, **not a claim that the original filmed material runs at 60 fps**. Preserve the intended display geometry and all HUD edges; do not stretch the game across a widescreen frame. OBS notes that unsupported resolution/FPS combinations can prevent capture. [OBS Video Capture Sources](https://obsproject.com/kb/video-capture-sources).

If the complete chain reliably supports a higher-resolution master, use it after the same checks; 1080p is a compatibility starting point, not a budget ceiling. Scaling does not recover detail missing from the original movie frames.

Record a clean game picture without a permanently baked facecam, timer or joke captions. Add those in the edit. Record a camera/phone angle separately if you want face or controller reactions, and make a visible/audible sync mark before play. Keep an untreated game master; an optional CRT-filter comparison is a separate shot.

Suggested audio routing:

| Track | Contents | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Game + microphone | Quick review mix |
| 2 | Game only | Dialogue, effects and clean game excerpts |
| 3 | Microphone only | Commentary level and edit control |

Use headphones to keep game audio out of the microphone. Choose a common sample rate such as 48 kHz across the recording devices. Record MKV, then remux to a format your editor accepts; test that the editor imports the isolated tracks. OBS documents MKV recording, remuxing and separate audio tracks. [Recording output guide](https://obsproject.com/kb/standard-recording-output-guide), [multi-track guide](https://obsproject.com/uk/wiki/advanced-recording-guide-with-multi-track-audio).

Before touching the campaign, capture a boot/title/intro sample and a microphone test. Inspect the **saved file**, not just the preview, for clipping, both audio channels, geometry and synchronization. Start the first gameplay capture before the first mission. Keep that footage as genuine first exposure; technical tests that include gameplay are not erased from the story.

### 2. Record the first exposure

- Read the basic control labels, but leave the manual's first-mission walkthrough and external playthroughs until after this attempt. If you already saw them, say so.
- Record the menu, full opening briefing, first commands, first confusion and first meaningful result. Let the game's spoken instructions finish before responding aloud.
- State what you think a command means. Genuine incorrect guesses make the later improvement understandable.
- After the first mission or failure, review a short saved sample before committing hours to capture. Log any restart caused by a recording problem.

### 3. Turn practice into a story

Plan a 60–90-minute learning block, then reassess; this is a work-session allowance, not an expected completion time. Record continuously while playing. Take a break or split learning sessions when useful, keeping the boundaries in the log.

Use a simple learning sheet: **what I expected → what happened → what I changed → what the next attempt proved**. Choose three recurring obstacles from real footage, such as missing radio responses, confusing selection with firing, or choosing the wrong weapon/mode. Do not force all three into the edit if they never happen.

During a sustained session, check the saved recording near its start and end for sync drift, dropouts and HUD readability. A valid short test does not establish a valid hour-long capture. Save a clean demonstration of each learned action for the explanation section.

### 4. Record the completion and final attempt

Finish the learning playthrough first. Keep the final mission, success sequence and credits intact in the master. Record a spoken reaction immediately, including whether this was a continuous campaign or a resumed learning clear.

If the main completion is already a satisfying ending, use it. If a second attempt gives the episode a stronger payoff, write the timing rules into the slate and record a fresh uninterrupted personal speed-clear. Give that attempt a distinct ID. Do not combine its best missions into a fictional continuous run.

Keep game audio and the original elapsed-time evidence. In the edited episode, show when time jumps and label the run as condensed. A clock left visible across cuts is not by itself proof of continuity. Keep the uncut attempt available for a companion upload if useful; an unlisted link is optional and must actually work before being promised.

### 5. Record pickups and narration last

Capture the hardware, disc, hands, relevant manual page, HUD demonstrations and optional version comparison. Re-created actions are demonstrations, not blind reactions. Write connective narration after you know the real outcome, then record it separately from the gameplay commentary.

## Episode outline

| Edit time | Beat | Footage and purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–0:25 | Cold open | A real missed command or failure, its consequence and your reaction. Establish the problem without revealing the campaign's final result. |
| 0:25–1:10 | The promise | Identify Tomcat Alley, the Sega CD pad and the back-seat role. State the completion challenge and allowed retries in one sentence. Keep the channel introduction brief. |
| 1:10–2:10 | What my job actually is | One briefing and an annotated HUD example. Explain A versus B through a visible action; avoid a full controls lecture. |
| 2:10–4:00 | First exposure | A coherent attempt: instruction, decision, result. Preserve at least one short sequence without interruption so viewers can understand the game. |
| 4:00–5:20 | The investigation | The useful manual discovery and one comparison between what you thought and what the interface expects. Use the actual mistake as the reason to explain it. |
| 5:20–7:20 | Training | Three examples of changed behavior if the footage supports them. Each mini-arc ends in a better result or an explained remaining problem. |
| 7:20–8:00 | Film craft or optional PC comparison | A brief relevant observation, not a mandatory diversion. Use your own matching captures if comparing editions. If this weakens the pace, remove it. |
| 8:00–11:30 | Completion/final attempt | Build around the remaining obstacle. Condense routine sections; let the last meaningful challenge and result breathe. If using a second run, tell viewers it is a separate attempt. |
| 11:30–13:00 | Verdict and scoreboard | Resolve the title question, show the actual result, say whether the interface became satisfying, and recommend an edition for this experience. |
| Last 10–15 seconds | Episode two | Tease Burning Soldier with a specific contrast: another filmed-action challenge, now with a different kind of shooting. |

These are edit allowances, not a 13-minute minimum. Extend only where the material earns it. Do not insert a long historical preamble before viewers understand the challenge.

### Draft host lines

**Opening:** “The good news is I don't have to fly the plane. The bad news is every other job is apparently mine.”

**The challenge:** “I'm going to finish Tomcat Alley on a Sega CD with this controller. The normal retries count. First I need to understand what the movie is asking me to do.”

**Investigation bridge:** “I thought I was missing the target. I was misunderstanding the job.” Use this only if the recorded mistake supports it; rewrite around the real discovery otherwise.

**Before the final attempt:** “I can explain the buttons now. Let's find out whether I can use them when everybody starts shouting.”

Write the verdict after recording. A win, an improvement with retries, and a clear explanation of failure require different endings. Never put invented attempt counts, deaths, timings or actor dialogue into the script.

## Fact card and source notes

The catalog snapshot lists **1994 · Sega · The Code Monkeys · FMV 10/10 · Titan rank 124, score 43.2** for the game family. Titan is this project's ranking, not a contemporary review score. Keep the card short; neither a price nor a leaderboard claim is needed to introduce episode one.

For a short film-production aside, the manual credits Sam Nicholson as director, John Zuur Platten as writer, Clay Lacy for aerial photography and The Code Monkeys for programming. It credits Grumman for Tomcat stock footage and Sikorsky for Blackhawk stock footage. Do not turn those acknowledgments into a claim of a US Navy license or an official Top Gun adaptation.
